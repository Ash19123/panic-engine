Educational model. This is a college project, not a real alert system and not a validated predictor. Safe stops are hypothetical, not official shelters. Nothing here sends a message to anyone.

The problem

When an alert goes out, everyone is told to go to a safe place at the same moment. They all choose the nearest exits and the same roads, so queues form and some people run out of time before the strike. How should people be routed, and how much warning time is needed, so that most reach safety in time?

PANIC ENGINE answers this on the road network of Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, Amaravati. In the simulation everyone receives the alert on their phone; what differs is the route they take and how early the alert comes.

How it works

Agents walk a road graph with capacity-limited roads and safe stops. Three routing strategies (shortest path, congestion-aware, load-balanced) are compared on identical crowds, and the alert time before a bomb strike can be varied. Every run is stored in SQLite and analysed with SQL. In our runs, congestion-aware routing got more people to safety than shortest-path routing (for example 100% against 81% in one run on the campus map); the numbers depend on assumed speeds, capacities and reaction delays.

Inspiration. We took inspiration from Carles, Chapuis, Douvinet, Péroche and Bopp (2024), “An Agent-Based Model to Simulate the Effects of Tsunami Warnings on Pedestrian Evacuation: Sensitivity Analysis and Early Findings”, PRIMA 2024, pp. 369–384, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-77367-9_28 (HAL hal-04794796). They study sirens versus cell broadcast and alert timing for a tsunami in Cannes. PANIC ENGINE is our own model for a bomb scenario; it is inspired by their questions and is not a replication of their work.
